Privacy Policy

The Short Version

Life is a place for your whole life, so it is built to keep what you put in it to yourself. Everything you write and add is stored on your iPhone. There's no account and no server of mine, so I never see any of it. Life has no analytics of its own, and it never asks to track you.

To do its work, Life asks a few services for answers — book covers, show and film details, place names, travel guides — and, unless you have Life Pro, it shows ads from Google. This page says what each of them gets.

Who Makes Life

Life is made by one person: Meftun Ege Kara, an independent developer in Türkiye. Because I decide how the app works, data protection law — KVKK in Türkiye, the GDPR in Europe — calls me its data controller. That doesn't mean I can see your information: I have no access to anything you keep in Life, because none of it ever comes to me.

You can write to me at meftunbusiness@gmail.com, or on Instagram at @meftunegekara.

What Stays on Your Phone

Everything you keep in Life is stored on your iPhone, in the app's own private space that other apps can't open:

  • what you write: notes, journal entries, letters, quotes, goals and events
  • what you keep track of: books, shows, films, workouts, body weight, measurements and progress photos
  • your trips, places and adventures, and what Passport finds in your photos — where your trips went, and where home is
  • your profile: the name, birthday and photo you chose to give
  • your home: its widgets, stickers, photos and backgrounds

None of this is sent to me, and Life keeps no copy of it anywhere else. Like every app's data, it is part of your iPhone's own backup — an iCloud Backup, or a backup on your computer — if you have one turned on. A few small details of it, such as a book's title or the spot where a photo was taken, go to the services below so they can answer; the next part says which.

What Leaves Your Phone, and Why

Some pages need answers from the internet. Life asks these services directly from your phone, over an encrypted connection. Each one gets only what it needs to answer, plus what any internet request carries: your IP address, and technical details such as your phone's language or system version. None of them gets your name, your notes, your journal or your photos.

  • Open Library, run by the Internet Archive, for books: what you type in book search; the title and author of a book in your library that has no cover yet, to find one; the Open Library number of a book whose page or other covers you open; and the names of up to four authors you love, have finished or are reading — and, on your first day, of the authors of the books you mark — to suggest more books. (Its policy)
  • TVmaze, for shows: what you type in show search, and the TVmaze number of each show you follow, to keep its episodes up to date. (Its policy)
  • Trakt, for films: what you type in film search; the number of each film you keep or open, and of a director whose page you open, to show their details and your country's opening dates; the kinds of film you watch most, such as Drama or Horror, to fill the genre rows; on your first day, the numbers of the films you mark, to show films like them; and, with Life Pro, the numbers of a few films you loved and of the directors you watch most, to suggest more. (Its policy)
  • Apple Maps, for places and maps: the place names you type; the maps and place cards you look at; for Passport and Adventures, the spots where your photos were taken — for Passport together with a word for what the photos there show, such as “beach” or “castle” — and the place you started a trip from, to name the towns and places and to draw the roads between them; and, for Life Pro's trip plans, the names of the places in the plan with their country, the restaurants near them and the roads between them. Your photos themselves are never sent. (Apple's policy)
  • Wikivoyage, Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons, for Life Pro's trip plans: the names of the country and the places in the plan, to fetch the travellers' guide and its photographs. (Their policy)
  • Google, for ads, unless you have Life Pro — see Ads, below.
  • The App Store, to check which Life Pro plan you have, and when you buy, restore or manage it.

When you tap Where to Watch, Life opens JustWatch's website with the film's title. When you open a place or a route in Apple Maps, the Maps app gets it. If you use iCloud Photos, some of your photos may live only in your iCloud and not on the phone itself. To show one of them, or to see what it shows, your iPhone fetches it from your own iCloud, the same way the Photos app does; it comes to your phone and goes nowhere else. The services above may keep what they receive in their own records. Each of them protects it under a privacy policy of its own, linked above, and Google — the only one whose software runs inside Life — does so under its data protection terms with app makers; I chose them for that, and send them nothing beyond what this page lists.

Ads

Without Life Pro, Life shows a few ads from Google AdMob, each one marked “Ad”. An ad request carries nothing of yours — no words, no titles, nothing you wrote or keep track of.

The ads aren't personalised: Life tells Google not to choose them by your interests, and because Life never asks to track you, Google doesn't get your phone's advertising identifier. To show and count ads and to stop fraud, Google's software still collects some information by itself — such as your IP address and the rough location it suggests, details about your phone, the ads you see and tap, and crash and performance data. To do so, Google and the ad companies it works with may store and read small pieces of data on your phone, such as cookies and identifiers — even for ads that aren't personalised, to limit how often you see one ad, to count them and to stop fraud. Google decides how it uses this as its own data controller, separately from me; it describes this in how Google uses information from apps that use its services.

Where the law asks for it — the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and some US states — Google's form asks for your choice the first time. You can change it any time in Settings → Privacy → Ad Privacy Choices.

With Life Pro there are no ads, and from the next time Life opens, Life doesn't start Google's ad software or ask it for anything.

What Your Phone Works Out by Itself

Some things are worked out by your phone alone, and nothing leaves it while they happen: the mark and colour suggested as you type a name, and the dishes and weather words of a trip plan, both written by Apple's on-device model; what your photos show, to choose the best of a trip and to know what kind of place to ask Apple Maps about — only that one word, such as “beach”, goes with the spot; where the face is in your profile photo, to frame it; and how you stand in your progress photos, to line them up.

What Life Asks For

Life asks for permission the first time a page needs it. You can change your answer any time in your phone's Settings, and see where each one stands in Life, in Settings → Privacy.

  • Photos — Passport reads where and when all your photos were taken, and what they show, worked out on your phone, to find your trips and your home; Adventures reads the day and place of the photos you add to an adventure, which stay in your library. Other photos you pick for a page are saved as Life's own copy, without the place they were taken.
  • Apple Music — your workout shows the song that's playing and lets you skip or pause it. Nothing about your music is saved.
  • Live Activities — the workout you're doing shows on your Lock Screen, where anyone holding your phone can see it.
  • Journaling Suggestions — Apple's picker shows moments from your days. Life only receives the one you pick — which can bring a place you went, a song, or a mood you logged in Health — and keeps it as a journal entry.

Apart from what a Journaling Suggestion you pick brings with it, Life doesn't use your phone's location, camera, microphone, contacts or calendars, doesn't read Apple Health, and doesn't send notifications.

Health and Fitness

Some of what you keep is about your health: your body weight and measurements, your progress photos, your workouts with any calories or heart rate you write in, and the moods in your journal — including one you logged in Apple Health and brought in through a Journaling Suggestion. It stays on your phone like everything else. It is never sent to anyone, and never used for ads or marketing.

Purchases

Life Pro is sold by Apple, through the App Store. Apple handles the payment and your Apple Account. Life only learns, on your phone, which plan you have and when it renews or ends, and keeps only which plan it is, on your phone. The sales reports Apple sends me don't say who bought.

Reports From Apple

If you've chosen to share analytics with app developers in your iPhone's settings, Apple sends me crash reports and anonymous numbers about how Life is used, such as how often it opens. They never hold what you keep in Life. You can turn this off in Settings → Privacy & Security → Analytics & Improvements.

Why This Is Allowed

Life handles your information to give you the app you chose to use and the things you ask it to do — under the GDPR, to perform our agreement (Article 6(1)(b)); under KVKK, because it is necessary for that agreement (Article 5(2)(c)). Where the law asks for consent to ads, they rely on the choice you make in Google's form (GDPR Article 6(1)(a)); elsewhere, on the legitimate interest of keeping Life free (GDPR Article 6(1)(f), KVKK Article 5(2)(f)).

Children

Life isn't made for children under 13. I don't knowingly collect information from children — I don't collect it from anyone. The ads are set to suit every age.

Keeping It Safe

Life's data sits in its private space on your iPhone, protected by your passcode and by iOS's own encryption, and every request Life makes goes over an encrypted (HTTPS) connection. There's more in Settings → Privacy → Security.

How Long Things Are Kept

What you add stays until you delete it; deleted notes wait in Recently Deleted for 30 days, and are removed the next time you open Notes after that. Covers and posters of the books, shows and films you keep are saved with them, and go when you delete them; other pictures from the services above — suggestions, search results, guide photos — are kept in a cache your phone may clear when it needs room. What Passport finds — your trips, the names Apple Maps gave their places, and what your photos show — stays on your phone until you delete it in Settings → Privacy → Forget What Passport Found. A trip plan stays until you delete it; the travel guide, dishes and weather it is made from are saved on your phone and fetched again when you open the plan after 30 days — with no connection, the saved copy simply stays — and they stay even after you delete the plan, until you delete Life. Deleting Life from your iPhone deletes everything it keeps there; only your own backups keep a copy.

Your Rights

Depending on where you live — KVKK in Türkiye (Article 11), the GDPR in Europe, the CCPA in California — you have the right to know what is held about you, to get a copy, to have it corrected or deleted, to object, and to take back a consent you gave. You don't have to give Life anything — your name, your birthday, your photos are all up to you — and Life makes no decisions about you by itself.

Because everything is on your phone, you can see, change and delete what you made yourself, in the app; Life doesn't have a way to export everything at once. What Passport works out from your photos can be deleted in Settings → Privacy → Forget What Passport Found; the travel guides saved for your trip plans go when you delete Life. I hold nothing about you to hand over or erase. For what a service above received, ask that service; for the ads, use Ad Privacy Choices or Google's own controls.

If you think something isn't right, please write to me first. You can also complain to a data protection authority — in Türkiye, the Personal Data Protection Authority; in the EU, the authority of your country.

Across Borders

Most of the services above — Apple, Google, the Internet Archive, Trakt and the Wikimedia Foundation — are based in the United States, so a request to them can travel abroad. It goes there directly from your phone, never through me.
